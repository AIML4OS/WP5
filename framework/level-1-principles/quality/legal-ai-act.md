The main objective of the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is to establish harmonised rules worldwide to ensure the reliable adoption of AI solutions. The key elements of the regulation are:

- The classification of the risk, to prevent harm.
- Clear definition of the roles and responsibilities of those involved.
- Guidelines and technical documentation for managing AI applications.

According to the level of risk and the type of processed information, different obligations and technical requirements must be complied, depending also on the specific role, whether AI providers and/or deployers. Specifically, organizations that develop or deploy AI systems falling under the high-risk category must:

- Establish a risk management system (Article 9) to identify, evaluate and mitigate potential harms.
- Ensure data governance quality (Article 10), including representativeness, relevance, and bias mitigation;
- Maintain technical documentation and record-keeping (Arts. 11–12) to support transparency and traceability.
- Implement human oversight mechanisms (Article 14) to ensure accountability in automated decision-making.
- Conduct a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA) for high-risk systems (Article 27), which is especially relevant for biometric or profiling applications.
- Comply with post-market monitoring and incident reporting obligations (Article 72).

Complying the AI Regulation to official statistics requires an assessment of the risks associated with specific tasks. In addition to the regulation, the risk is assessed in relation to the responsible AI principles and the role of AI within a specific use case. AI solutions may bring different degrees of automation and human interaction, namely (Piela, 2024):

- Autonomous AI – total automation and minimal human involvement.
- Collaborative AI – partial automation and human overseeing.
- Supportive AI – AI helps increase human efficiency.

Different use cases may involve different levels of risk due to their specific characteristics. However, when creating best practices for official statistics, it is recommended that tasks are not fully automated, but rather that human intervention is provided for monitoring and review purposes. Therefore, a flexible, principle-based approach to complying with the AI Act seems most appropriate for official statistics, given that its implementation is still evolving across countries and institutional settings. Rather than creating a separate compliance framework, where possible, the requirements of the AI Act could be integrated alongside existing ESS quality and governance processes, in particular the European Statistics Code of Practice (CoP) and the Quality Assurance Framework (QAF), as well as established internal methodological and quality review procedures. This approach helps to avoid duplication and supports coherence between legal compliance, methodological soundness, and quality assurance.

In relation to the statistical domain, regardless of the GSBPM[1] phase in which AI is integrated, in compliance with the AI act and the responsible AI principles, transparency is mandatory. In line with the recommendation to apply clear and visible labelling to certain AI-generated content, the variables/records processed through AI solutions should be flagged and disseminated via the quality report.

## Risk categories

In relation to risk prevention, the AI Act introduces a risk-based regulatory framework which classifies AI systems into one of four categories: unacceptable risk (prohibited), high risk (regulated), limited risk (subject to transparency obligations) and minimal risk (unregulated). In order to comply with the AI Act, it is necessary to assess the risks associated with the use of AI solutions in the statistical process. ## General-purpose AI models

At first analysis, these applications appear to fall under the category of General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models, which are clearly regulated in the AI Act. This provides a foundation for many AI systems in the EU, ensuring that AI is secure and reliable. In order to grant compliance to the obligations established for GPAI models, the AI act provides a set of guidelines, the GPAI Code of Practice and a Template for the public summary of training content of GPAI models and the description of processing steps.

## Wider governance context

In order to strengthen the integration of AI requirements in official statistics, it is also helpful to analyse the AI Act alongside references to broader governance, such as the UNECE Responsible AI Framework for Official Statistics, the OECD AI Principles and the relevant ISO/IEC standards on AI governance. While these sources do not replace the ESS framework, they help to position the proposed approach within a wider international policy context, reinforcing the emphasis on accountability, transparency, robustness, and lifecycle governance. Last but not least, NSIs may gain benefits from the various initiatives launched at European level, for example the “Apply AI Strategy”[2] and the “Data Union Strategy”. The goal of these implementation actions is respectively: to promote the adoption and integration of AI solutions in the main public and private sectors, and guarantee the availability of high-quality, large-scale datasets for training AI models.

## Summary and timeline

In summary, beyond the ongoing changes, the AI Act reinforces the need for the ethical, transparent and accountable use of AI in public sector data ecosystems. NSIs must proactively assess their AI systems and prepare for compliance to maintain trust and legal conformity within the evolving European AI governance landscape. Concerning the implementation actions, the AI Act also sets out a phased compliance timeline:

- August 2024: Entry into force;
- February 2025: Prohibited AI systems are banned;
- August 2026: Obligations for high-risk AI systems (Annex III) come into effect;
- August 2027: Full compliance will be required for systems placed on the market before August 2025.

In the meantime, the European Statistical System (ESS) must carry out a detailed analysis of the implications of the AI Act for the field of statistics. This is necessary for AI to be lawfully endorsed in official statistics.
