## General considerations

The rapid advancement of AI/ML is reshaping how NSIs collect, process, analyse and disseminate information. While these technologies can enhance efficiency, quality and timeliness, their use must remain fully aligned with the values of official statistics including professional independence, impartiality, objectivity, transparency, confidentiality and accountability. These values are enshrined in national and European statistical legislation and international frameworks such as the UN Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics (FPOS), the European Statistics Code of Practice (CoP) etc.

AI/ML is neither inherently ethical nor unethical, and its impact will reflect factors such as design choices, training data, governance arrangements and implementation practices. While discussion often focuses on specific production phases such as GSBPM 4 to 6, ethical risks may also arise in design and dissemination, particularly with the emergence of Generative AI, but these are outside the scope of the document.

Sustainable benefits can only be achieved if AI/ML is deployed within clear ethical boundaries and in service of the public good. Since official statistics depend fundamentally on public trust and cooperation, openness about how AI/ML is applied, what safeguards exist, and how risks are mitigated is essential.

## AI and trust in official statistics

Trust is central to the legitimacy of official statistics and to their acceptance and anything that could undermine this trust must therefore be carefully managed. Public scepticism regarding AI is increasing in some contexts and this should be acknowledged and transparently addressed. The use of AI in NSIs represents more than a continuation of traditional data processing advances; it signals a shift from rule-based programming towards adaptive, self-learning systems. Moreover, as AI tools gain greater autonomy, they may carry out functions without direct or visible human intervention.

AI can be associated with misinformation, opacity and automation bias. Citizens may fear that statistics are “generated by AI” or involve “black-box magic” rather than being grounded in verified data and professional judgement. Even internal statistical uses such as coding or imputation may prompt concerns about reduced human oversight of these processes because, unlike traditional software, AI is often perceived as an autonomous decision-maker. Other AI concerns include discrimination, profiling, surveillance risks, and even the environmental impact of AI usage. For example, AI models trained on historically biased data may inadvertently perpetuate or amplify those biases. A single high-profile error involving AI could damage the credibility of a statistical product or even the NSI itself.

According to the European Commission’s High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence (AI HLEG) 2019 Ethical Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, AI usage should be lawful, ethical and robust. Lawfulness requires adherence to applicable legislation; ethical alignment ensures consistency with fundamental values; and robustness demands both technical reliability and social resilience. Embedding these elements in AI use will reinforce the role of NSIs as trusted and authoritative providers of information. It will demonstrate a commitment to responsible AI innovation and use, reassure the public that safeguards are in place, and empower NSI staff to challenge inappropriate AI uses.

## International guidelines for the use of AI in official statistics

While direct comparisons can be complex due to differing legal and institutional contexts, several jurisdictions/statistical offices have already published AI-specific guidance for usage in statistics, including the following:

- Statistics Canada's Framework for Responsible Machine Learning Processes (https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/catalogue/892000062021001)
- UK Statistics Authority's Ethical considerations in the use of Machine Learning for research and statistics (https://uksa.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/publication/ethical-considerations-in-the-use-of-machine-learning-for-research-and-statistics/pages/1/)
- UNECE's International Framework on Responsible AI for Official Statistics (https://unece.org/statistics/documents/2025/10/reports/responsible-ai-official-statistics-framework)
- Destatis paper on ethical issues concerning the use of AI/ML in the production of official statistics (https://www.destatis.de/EN/Methods/WISTAScientificJournal/Downloads/ethical-issues-concerning-the-use-of-ai-ml-part1-identification-012025.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=3, https://www.destatis.de/EN/Methods/WISTAScientificJournal/Downloads/ethical-issues-concerning-the-use-of-ai-ml-part2-evaluation-012025.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=3)
- Statistics Sweden's AI Policy (https://www.scb.se/contentassets/99b579ae03d245c1a5700c12947746cf/ai-policy_20240611_en.pdf)

These documents show strong convergence around principles such as human oversight, fairness, transparency, robustness, proportionality and accountability. Some countries integrate AI risks within existing legal and quality systems while others adopt dedicated AI frameworks. This suggests that AI governance can either complement or be embedded within established structures, provided gaps are addressed. This of course depends on the ethical risks which arise with the use of AI.
